Social Justice

My preliminary Grant Proposal for an imagined digital community archive project for SLIS 5400: Community Archives aligns with the ALA Core Competence on Social Justice. My imagined archive, CACAO (Capoeira America Community Archive Online), serves as a centralized hub for preserving the intangible and ephemeral traditions of US capoeiristas. This project demonstrates how to transition a conceptual blueprint into an operational archival project that serves an underdocumented community.

If the PDF Viewer doesn't render for you, view my Grant Proposal here.

Through this assignment, I recognized and intentionally challenged dominant archival systems that often marginalize decentralized Afro-Brazilian cultural expressions (8B). I also designed a social justice framework that relies on outreach and partnerships, using a Community Advisory Board to ensure the community retains participation in and control over its narrative (8D). I learned that addressing archival silences requires moving beyond institutional ownership models toward stewardship, in which the archive serves as a digital steward rather than a proprietor.

This project has influenced my professional growth by teaching me to balance theoretical activism with the realistic constraints of labor and infrastructure. To improve my future practice, I will consider how to lower barriers for marginalized groups to document their own experiences in real time, such as through mobile digitization kits. I will prioritize building inclusive collections that recognize practitioners of a tradition as the rightful authorities over their own historical records.

Professional Development

On August 26, 2026, I attended the live guest speaker session for the Library Journal course "Everyone Deserves a Library: Serving Patrons Experiencing Homelessness (Summer 2026)." The speaker was Kavan Stafford, a librarian in Scotland who has conducted research and written about homelessness and public libraries. He shared data from other publications and his own research on poverty and homelessness in the US and UK, and how those factors shape people's experiences in public libraries. Stafford's overarching theme was that homeless patrons are library users with information needs; he noted that many staff need better training and preparation to serve them more effectively and with more compassion, and many libraries need to rethink their policies on how they treat homeless patrons.

3 Takeaways

  1. The biggest barriers in the lives of homeless patrons in libraries are the attitudes of library staff. A distinction has to be drawn between feeling unsafe and being unsafe. This can be addressed through training.
  2. Homeless people's information needs are often underserved, and their rights to access libraries are undefined. A patron's housing status is not the most important thing about providing library services to that person. The most commonly reported information needs, regardless of housing status, are:
    • Employment
    • Financial
    • Use of computers
    • Internet access
  3. As librarians, our job is to help homeless users access the library fairly and equitably. No rules should be made except regarding apparent or actual harm. Library staff don't kick users out for being over-perfumed or kick out toddlers for being smelly or sleeping. Doing so to homeless patrons is due to bias and prejudice.

2 Questions

  1. Why do some public library staff, who signed up for a job working with the public, seem surprised and dismayed when they actually have to work with the public, especially the people who most need the services public libraries offer?
  2. If the top reported information needs include employment and financial information resources, why are those resources not more readily available at more public libraries? Why is there not more targeted training in those areas for librarians?

1 Thing

One thing I started doing after this session is responding when someone comments about homeless people in the library, either generally or about a specific patron's appearance or behavior. I respond in different ways depending on whether the commenter is a patron or another staff member.

If it's a patron, I look at them directly and say, "Everyone is welcome in the library." If the patron is expressing discomfort, I offer to help them find a more comfortable space, like a table in a quiet corner or, if available, a study room.

If the commenter is a staff member, I ask them to explain exactly how the patron's behavior violates the Patron Code of Conduct. It rarely is, and the complaint is something like the patron might be making people uncomfortable. To comments like that, I respond that the patron is allowed to exist in the library, and it's not our job to assume how other patrons might feel.
