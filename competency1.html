1. Gateway Knowledge

My "casting off essay" for SLIS 5010: Libraries, Culture, and Society exemplifies my engagement with the ALA Gateway Knowledge Competence. This paper explores the intersection of library policies, restorative justice practices, and how public libraries treat youth patrons, particularly teens of color. It embodies key competencies, including the ethics and values of librarianship (1A), democratic principles and intellectual freedom (1B), and the historical and social contexts of libraries (1C, 1F). This assignment allowed me to develop and demonstrate my ability to analyze complex problems and propose collaborative solutions (1I) while engaging in effective written communication (1J).

For this paper, I examined how teens, particularly those from marginalized communities, often face over-policing and exclusion in library spaces. This process required deep engagement with foundational principles of the library profession, such as equity of access and intellectual freedom (1A, 1B). I traced libraries' historical role as both sites of inclusion and exclusion (1C). I analyzed current trends, such as the rise of restorative justice practices, to frame my arguments within a broader social and cultural context (1F). By critically evaluating library policies and case studies, I identified barriers that hinder equitable access for teens and proposed practical strategies to create more inclusive environments (1I).

This research shaped my professional perspective and future practice, reinforcing that libraries are dynamic institutions that must continually adapt to meet the needs of diverse patron populations. As a result, I have developed a more nuanced understanding of how libraries can serve as catalysts for social change.

Professional Development

On February 10, 2026, I attended Recharging in Challenging Times: An ALA Virtual Event, an all-day online mini-conference hosted by the American Library Association to bolster information professionals facing so many stressors like burnout, political trauma, budget woes, and the day-to-day demands of working with the public. The closing keynote in particular stuck with me. It was a conversation between then-ALA President Sam Helmick and Robert Newlen, Acting Librarian of Congress, titled "Mission vs. Means: Staying True to the Mission with Ever-Changing Resources." They discussed Newlen's transition into the role of Librarian of Congress after Carla Hayden's dismissal, how the work goes on even in challenging times, and new initiatives planned at the Library of Congress.

3 Takeaways

  1. Everybody needs a mentor, because we never stop growing. We never stop learning from or being inspired by others. We need to turn to each other and learn from what is happening in different fields of librarianship.
  2. Librarians offer context and curation to content. That's what makes our work so important, so valuable. We help people filter out misinformation, bias, and predatory sources so they can find and access reliable information and transform it into meaningful knowledge.
  3. A paraphrased quote from Robert Newlen (copied from my notes): Be kind to yourself. You won't always deliver the performance you expect, and that's okay. If you accomplish 80% of what you wanted, you've been 100% successful.

2 Questions

  1. How can libraries/librarians better communicate the value they provide to those outside of the field so that our institutions stop coming under attack from censorship and funding threats?
  2. How do people find mentors they click with once they're in the field? I've been assigned mentors before, in my previous career, and it always felt like I was a checkbox on someone's to-do list.

1 Thing

One thing I started doing because of this event is not pushing to finish everything on my to-do lists each day. Newlen's quote stayed with me, and I realized that wearing myself out rushing to do all the things by a (sometimes) arbitrary deadline wasn't really increasing my impact; it was just making me tired and overwhelmed.
